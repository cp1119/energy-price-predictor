energy-price-predictor · DE-LU day-ahead

How a power price is set, and how this repo predicts it

Part 1 explains the market from first principles. Part 2 walks through the code: data, features, models, and how they are scored. Each idea gets an engineering analogy where one helps.

Part 1 · The market

The product: energy for one quarter-hour, delivered tomorrow

Electricity can't be stored cheaply at grid scale, so supply must match demand every instant. Europe handles most of this with a daily auction called the day-ahead market (SDAC). Every day at 12:00 CET, buyers and sellers submit bids for each delivery period of the next day. Since 1 October 2025 those periods are 15 minutes long, so each day has 96 separate prices. Your repo marks this switch with the feature is_qh_era.

The merit order: sort plants by running cost

Each generator offers its output at roughly its marginal cost: the cost of producing one more MWh. For wind and solar that is close to zero (no fuel). For a gas plant it is mostly fuel plus CO₂ permits. The exchange sorts all offers from cheapest to dearest, which gives a staircase supply curve. Where the staircase crosses demand is the clearing price, and every seller in that period gets paid that price ("pay-as-clear").

Analogy: it is like a pump curve meeting a system curve. The operating point is wherever they intersect. Shift either curve and the operating point moves. Wind and solar shift the supply curve right; demand shifts the vertical line.

Residual load: the one number that explains most of the price

Residual load = demand − wind − solar. It is the demand left over for the plants that cost money to run. Low residual load means the cheap part of the staircase is enough, so the price is low. High residual load pushes the crossing point up into gas and oil, so the price jumps. The staircase is steep at the top, which is why prices are spiky and non-linear. Your feature table builds this directly: residual_load = load − wind − solar.

Why prices go negative

When wind plus solar exceed demand, some generators still bid below zero: subsidised plants that earn per MWh produced, and inflexible plants for which shutting down and restarting costs more than paying someone to take power for a few hours. The model has to handle these negative values, which is why LEAR uses an asinh transform (see Part 2).

Neighbours and coupling

All European zones clear in one coupled algorithm, constrained by interconnector capacity. If France has cheap nuclear and the lines are not full, power flows to Germany and prices converge. If the lines are congested, prices split. Neighbour prices from yesterday are therefore a good hint about tomorrow's flows. Note the repo can only use neighbours' prices up to day D, because all zones publish D+1 at the same moment.

The daily timeline

D, ~09:00TSOs publish day-ahead load forecasts for D+1. D, 10:00Your model's decision time (DECISION_HOUR = 10). Forecast made with what is known now. D, 12:00Gate closure. Bids locked. D, ~12:4596 prices for D+1 published for every zone. D, 18–21:00TSO wind/solar forecasts for D+1 appear on ENTSO-E (after gate closure, see caveat at the end). D+1Delivery. Intraday and balancing markets fix the remaining errors.

Step 2 · Features (features.py)

Every series is reshaped into a day × slot matrix: one row per local Berlin day, 96 columns for the quarter-hours. That makes "same quarter-hour yesterday" a simple row shift. Step 3 · Models (models/)

Benchmarks. naive copies yesterday's prices (or last week's for Mon/Sat/Sun, where yesterday was a different kind of day). Any model must beat this to be worth anything. seasonal_naive always copies last week.

LEAR (lear.py, from Lago et al. 2021, the standard academic benchmark). It fits 96 separate linear regressions, one per quarter-hour. Inputs are 199 hourly values: prices of D, D−1, D−2, D−6, tomorrow's load, wind, solar, today's residual load, and weekday dummies. Three tricks:

  • Lasso: an L1 penalty pushes useless coefficients to exactly zero, so the model picks a few dozen inputs out of 199. The penalty strength is chosen by AIC.
  • Robust scaling + asinh: each input is centred on its median and scaled by its MAD, then passed through asinh. asinh acts like a log for big values but passes smoothly through zero and works for negatives, so price spikes don't dominate the fit.
  • Window averaging: it fits on the last 56, 84, 364 and 728 days and averages the four. Short windows react to new regimes; long ones are stable.

LightGBM (gbm.py). A single gradient-boosted tree model for all slots, with slot and hour as features. Each tree is a set of if/else splits ("residual load > 45 GW and hour = 18 → +40 €"). 800 small trees are added one at a time, each correcting the errors of the ones before. Trees capture the kinked, staircase shape of the merit order that a linear model can't.

  • Objective l1: minimises absolute error, so a single crazy spike doesn't drag the whole model.
  • Recency weights: a training day loses half its weight every 180 days (half_life_days), so 2022 gas-crisis behaviour fades.
  • Tuned hyperparameters came from Optuna on a validation year (scripts/tune_gbm.py → results/gbm_best_params.json). It sees only 31% of features per tree and requires 635 rows per leaf, which keeps it from memorising noise.
  • The code can optionally predict price minus yesterday's mean, because trees can't extrapolate beyond prices they have seen. Tuning chose to switch this off (relative_to_level: false).

Ensemble. forecast_tomorrow.py averages LEAR and LightGBM. Their errors are only partly correlated (one linear, one tree-based), so the average is usually better than either.

Caveats to keep in mind

  • Wind/solar timing. The backtest treats D+1 wind/solar forecasts as known at 10:00, but ENTSO-E publishes them after gate closure. Live, the script warns if they're missing. Backtest errors are therefore somewhat optimistic. Using a weather-based forecast available in the morning would fix this.
  • Regime changes. Gas prices and CO₂ are not inputs. If gas doubles, the model only learns it through rising lagged prices, a day or more late.
  • Point forecast only. For battery dispatch, the shape (peak-minus-trough spread) matters more than the level, and uncertainty bands would help. Quantile objectives in LightGBM are a natural next step.
